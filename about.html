About & Leadership Philosophy

How I think about finance, controls, and trust

Six years of technical depth, and the judgment principles that came with it.

I'm an ACCA member and SOCPA Associate based in Al Khobar, Saudi Arabia, with six years of experience across financial accounting, cost control, and compliance in multi-entity, multi-currency trading operations. My work has sat at the intersection of two things that don't always get connected: technical rigor (IFRS, ZATCA, Zakat, internal controls) and practical business impact (working capital, vendor cost accuracy, audit readiness). I'm bilingual in Arabic and English, which has meant being the actual bridge between finance, operations, procurement, HR, banks, and external auditors — not just processing transactions between them.

In December 2025, I was awarded the ACCA AAA Prize for the highest mark in Saudi Arabia in Advanced Audit & Assurance — a result I'm proud of not because of the mark itself, but because of what earning it required: genuinely understanding why controls and assurance work the way they do, not just how to pass an exam on them.

That same question — why, not just how — is what's driving my next move. I've spent six years making sure the numbers are accurate, compliant, and audit-ready. I now want to spend the next phase of my career making sure those numbers change decisions.

Leadership Philosophy — four operating principles

Grounded in real, verified situations from my own work. I have not formally managed a team — these are judgment principles built through cross-functional and process-ownership work, not people-management experience.

1. Controls should protect decisions, not slow them down

When I maintained segregation of duties and independently verified supplier bank-detail changes, the instinct from outside finance was often that this was bureaucratic friction. I've come to see it differently: a control that's designed well doesn't slow a business down — it protects the speed the business already has, by making sure a bad decision doesn't have to be unwound later.

2. Investigate the evidence before you assume the story

Reconciling supplier invoice discrepancies through three-way matching taught me a habit I now apply everywhere: a mismatch is not evidence of wrongdoing until you've actually completed the reconciliation. I've learned to hold that discipline even under pressure to close a period quickly.

3. Working capital is a cross-functional conversation, not an accounting exercise

Reducing receivables ageing by 30% didn't happen inside the finance function alone — it required changing how credit terms were agreed with customers, how collections were prioritized, and how operations and sales understood the cash impact of their decisions. The result looked like an accounting metric, but the work was business partnering.

4. Trust is built by calling things accurately, not by avoiding conflict

Working across finance, procurement, operations, HR, banks, and auditors means regularly being the one who has to say "this doesn't reconcile yet." I've found that consistently calling it accurately, rather than smoothing it over, is what actually earns cross-functional trust over time.
